Jari ya Hamouda

جاري يا حمودة
“My Neighbor Hamouda” (also known as Jari ya Hamouda... Hamouda)

A vibrant and tender traditional Tunisian love and courtship song from the Maghreb, preserved and arranged by Chris Gaca for intercultural choral singing. The song depicts village life and a lover’s restless devotion for neighbor Hamouda, set to infectious North African rhythms.

Language: Tunisian Arabic (Derja)
Field / Performance Video: Confirmed (YouTube)
Transliteration & Translation: Full English & Latin Phonetics
Source Document: Original Word (.doc)
PROVENANCE & DISCOVERY

Chris’s Journey with the Song

Chris Gaca first encountered Jari ya Hamouda while traveling in Morocco for the renowned World Sacred Music Festival in Fez. Hearing folk legend Joan Baez perform the song live ignited Chris's fascination with its melody and spirit.

However, Joan Baez sang only the opening verse in authentic Arabic, shifting into a colloquial Franco-Arabic blend for subsequent verses. Seeking the pure Tunisian folk roots, Chris conducted research to locate the authentic lyrics, discovering that the chorus standard traced back to the celebrated recording by Tunisian singer Lora Khalil, which became the definitive reference for the choral arrangement.

“

The translation was also somewhat difficult to obtain, but we did manage to get a translation from Roset Khosropour’s friend Denna. Some of the words were still a little bit hard to distinguish, so our wonderful Roset worked with me to look again at the original Arabic words and make sure our version was accurate. Some words in our transliteration are spelled out in a way to make them more western friendly in terms of pronunciation.

— Chris Gaca, Curatorial Notes, WordPress Archive (August 31, 2012)

Through this collaborative work with native speakers and linguistic friends, Chris preserved both the nuanced emotional meaning of the Arabic poetry and a practical phonetic stepping-stone for Western choir singers learning non-Western vocal literature.

PEDAGOGICAL ARCHIVE

Translation, Lyrics & Pronunciation

In accordance with Chris’s teaching methodology, the Living Archive structures vocal resources across three interlinked tiers: the original poetic script, singer-tailored Latin transliteration, and evocative English translations. Below is a comparative excerpt from the verified archive manuscript:

Original Arabic Singer's Transliteration English Poetic Translation
جاري يا حمودة... يا جاري دبر عليا يا مّا
Jari ya Hamouda... ya jari daber aâliya yamma
“Hamouda my neighbor... Oh my neighbor, tell me what to do, mother”
ناس تبات رقودة واني نومي محروم عليا يا مّا
Ness tebet ergouda... wani noumi makharoum aâliya yamma
“While others sleep peacefully, sleep is stolen away from me”
حمّودة يا جاري... إنت طفّيلي ناري يا مّا
Hamouda ya jari... enta ti li mashaa-li nari yamma
“Hamouda my neighbor... it is you who feeds this fire in my heart”
تمنيتك في داري... مثلي شمعة تطل عليا يا مّا
Tmanitek fi dari... mesthli chamma ettol aaleya yamma
“I dream of you coming to my home, like a candle to bring light upon me”
★ Transliterated and translated by Chris Gaca with linguistic collaboration from Roset Khosropour and Denna.

Vocal & Pronunciation Notes for Choirs

  • The aâ (ع - Ayn): A voiced pharyngeal fricative. Chris noted that Western singers can approximate this by slightly opening the throat and lengthening the open vowel sound without tightening.
  • The kh (خ - Kha): A soft voiceless uvular fricative (as in the German Bach or Scottish loch). Kept gentle so as not to disrupt melodic legato.
  • Refrain Phrasing: The call of “Hamouda... Hamouda” acts as a communal pulse, ideal for layered choral responses and rhythmic hand-clapping typical of Maghrebi social folk gatherings.
